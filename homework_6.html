01 / THEORY

Independence in probability and statistics

Two categorical variables are independent when knowing the category of one variable does not change the probability distribution of the other.

What does independence mean?

Let X and Y be categorical variables. They are independent if, for every possible pair of categories x and y, the joint probability equals the product of the corresponding marginal probabilities:

P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) × P(Y = y)

An equivalent way to express the same idea is that the conditional distribution of Y is unchanged when we know X:

P(Y = y | X = x) = P(Y = y)

The same condition can also be written with X and Y reversed. In a sample, we investigate independence by comparing observed cell counts with the counts that would be expected if the variables were independent.

Null hypothesis · H₀
Independent
No association between the categorical variables in the population.
Alternative · H₁
Associated
At least one joint probability differs from the independence model.
Independence is not causality. A statistical association does not show that one variable causes the other. Confounding variables, selection effects, reverse relationships, or coincidence may explain an observed association.

04 / TEST

Expected frequencies and Pearson’s χ²

Under the null hypothesis of independence, each expected cell count is calculated from its row total and column total. The test measures how far the observed counts depart from that model.

Expected frequencies under independence

Eᵢⱼ = (Row totalᵢ × Column totalⱼ) / N

The expected value is the count we would anticipate in a cell if X and Y were independent, while keeping the observed marginal totals fixed.

05 / INTERPRET

What does the result tell us?

A hypothesis test provides evidence about whether the observed relationship is compatible with independence. It does not explain why that relationship exists.

How to interpret the p-value

The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of obtaining a chi-square statistic at least as large as the one calculated from the sample. Compare it with the chosen significance level α:

When p-value ≤ α
Reject H₀
The sample provides evidence of an association between X and Y.
When p-value > α
Do not reject H₀
There is insufficient evidence of an association in this sample.

Statistical association versus causality

A significant chi-square result indicates that the distribution of one variable differs across categories of the other. It does not prove that changing X would cause a change in Y. An unmeasured third variable might influence both, or the data collection process might create an apparent relationship.

To make a causal claim, a researcher needs additional justification, such as a suitable randomized experiment or a defensible causal research design that addresses confounding and alternative explanations.
