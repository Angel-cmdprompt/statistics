Running mean and variance, and how samples behave

Part 1 derives Welford’s update rules step by step. Part 2 puts them to work in a simulation of hackers attacking systems, to see what sample statistics do as the sample grows.

Part 1 · Welford’s update rules

Let x̄k be the average of the first k values, SSk = Σ(xi − x̄k)² their squared deviations, and s²k = SSk/(k−1) the sample variance for k ≥ 2. Write δk = xk − x̄k−1 for how far the new value lands from the old mean.

Updating the mean

Multiplying out the definition gives k·x̄k = (k−1)·x̄k−1 + xk, so the new mean is a small correction of the old one:

x̄k = x̄k−1 + δk / k

Two handy consequences: x̄k − x̄k−1 = δk/k, and xk − x̄k = δk·(k−1)/k.

Updating the sum of squares

Start from SSk = Σxi² − k·x̄k² and subtract the same identity for k−1. What remains is

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·x̄k² + (k−1)·x̄k−1²

Because (k−1)·x̄k−1 = k·x̄k − xk, the last term can be rewritten, and everything collapses into

SSk − SSk−1 = (xk − x̄k−1)(xk − x̄k)
SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − x̄k−1)(xk − x̄k) = SSk−1 + ((k−1)/k)·δk²,   SS1 = 0

Updating the variance

Replace SSk by (k−1)s²k and SSk−1 by (k−2)s²k−1, then divide by k−1:

s²k = ((k−2)/(k−1))·s²k−1 + δk² / k   (k ≥ 2),   s²2 = δ2²/2

Every term added here is about the size of the spread in the data. The textbook shortcut Σx² − k·x̄² behaves differently: it subtracts two enormous, almost identical numbers whenever the mean dwarfs the spread, and the digits that matter vanish.

Implementation

function welford(data) {
  let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
  for (const x of data) {
    k++;
    const d = x - mean;       // delta_k = x_k - mean_{k-1}
    mean += d / k;            // new mean
    ss   += d * (x - mean);   // SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - mean_{k-1})(x_k - mean_k)
  }
  return { mean, ss, variance: ss / (k - 1) };
}

Enter values separated by commas. The default list has a huge mean and a tiny spread, which is exactly where the shortcut formula struggles. The exact variance of the default list is 1.6667.

Why the one-pass sums formula fails

The expression (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1) is correct on paper. In floating point, though, both terms are gigantic and nearly equal when the spread is small, so their difference can be dominated by rounding error, sometimes even coming out negative.

Part 2 · Sample mean and variance in action

Each of m hackers attacks n systems and earns a score from 0 to 10 per system, drawn from one fixed distribution. Welford’s rules give each hacker an x̄ and an s² over the n scores, and then those results are averaged across all m hackers. Every hacker produces one long run of 10000 scores, and the statistics are read off at n = 10, 100, 1000 and 10000.

What the numbers say

Averaged over hackers, x̄ lands on μ and s² lands on σ² at every n, even n = 10. These estimators are unbiased: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². The n−1 divisor is what makes that true. Dividing by n instead gives ((n−1)/n)·σ² on average, a shortfall that is plain at small n and visible in the SS/n column. Whatever gaps remain are simulation noise, and they close as m grows. The coverage column hovers near 95%; it starts a bit lower for small n because 1.96 assumes a normal shape and s is itself noisy, then climbs toward 0.95.

This matters because μ and σ² are never known in practice, while x̄ and s² come straight from the data and are right on average. With Var(x̄) = σ²/n, the standard error s/√n is available, and confidence intervals and tests are built on it.

Growing n does not move the averages, but it tightens them. The spread of x̄ falls like σ/√n, so a hundred times more systems means ten times more precision. The spread of s² also falls like 1/√n, with a bigger constant, √(μ₄ − σ⁴). Pinning down a variance depends on the fourth moment, so it is always harder than pinning down a mean at the same n.
